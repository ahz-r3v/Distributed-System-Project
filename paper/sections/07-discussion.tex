\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The two studies support a common design lesson: recovery and scheduling should account for where reusable state resides.
Prefix-aware ordering substantially improves replication means, yet worsens some request tails and changes predictive eviction's effect.
Host restoration reduces missing-prefix work in the reported replay, while additional policy differences are smaller and less thoroughly repeated.
Neither study establishes a universally best policy.

External validity is bounded by one 14B model, Blackwell hardware, synthetic content, selected workloads, and implementation-specific replay rules.
The replication retains a declared preemption deviation with unmeasured extra computation; the host analysis reproduces rounded reported observations rather than independently auditing all request records.
The studies' timing and token metrics remain separate.
Restoration adds host memory, and nominal KV capacities exclude other process memory.

Offloading can lose when prefixes are short, reuse is unlikely, transfers contend, or the backing tier thrashes.
An SSD extension must measure full save-and-restore cost, including staging, filesystem caching, and queueing, rather than extrapolate from DRAM copies.
Persistent metadata, storage I/O, and failure recovery remain future implementation work.
