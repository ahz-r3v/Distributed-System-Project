\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and Problem}
Coding agents alternate language-model inference with external operations such as reading files, running commands, and editing code.
Successive requests can share a growing prompt prefix, making its attention key--value (KV) state valuable across turns~\cite{Tiwari2026CacheWise}.
During tool execution, however, keeping that state on the GPU consumes capacity that other requests could use.
Evicting it releases memory but creates a recovery decision when the agent resumes: recompute the missing prefix or restore a compatible saved copy.
InferCept already uses interruption information to guide preservation, CPU swapping, and recomputation~\cite{Abhyankar2024InferCept}.

CacheWise combines prefix-aware scheduling with predictive eviction based on tool-call metadata, and also evaluates GPU--CPU offloading~\cite{Tiwari2026CacheWise}.
We first examine these scheduling and eviction mechanisms in an adapted replay, then ask how recovery should change when GPU and CPU memory are both bounded.
SSDs offer another storage tier, but their capacity alone does not establish a serving-time benefit.
Writing KV state, staging transfers through host memory, and restoring it later can cost more than recomputation, especially when reuse is uncertain or transfers compete with active requests.
Systems such as CachedAttention already support hierarchical KV storage~\cite{Gao2024CachedAttention}; our objective is to characterize when that approach is useful for the replicated coding-agent workload and what additional policy decisions the evidence supports.

\subsection{Research Questions and Contributions}
We organize the study around three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How much of CacheWise's serving benefit comes from scheduling and eviction in our adapted setup?
  \item When does a complete offload-and-restore cycle cost less than recovering the same missing prefix through recomputation, with DRAM restoration as an intermediate baseline?
  \item Can tool-return information guide SSD placement and transfer timing to reduce exposed recovery delay without excessive I/O work?
\end{enumerate}

The completed contribution is an adapted replication comprising an initial six-policy experiment with 18 runs and a separate 60-run coverage study with 6,384 request executions.
The coverage study spans nested $N=10,20,30$ workloads and two additional held-out cohorts.
Our primary metric is mean session serving time, excluding external tool execution.
Prefix-aware LRU achieves the lowest mean in all five coverage settings; adding predictive eviction increases it by 10.07--43.38\%, although predictive eviction improves FCFS.
Full eviction-event counts support the setting-level ordering, while request-tail measurements show that lower session means do not ensure lower P99 latency.
Historical oracle and queue-aging diagnostics further distinguish predictor effects from behavior of the local scheduler.
The coverage study retains one observed preemption under a declared audit amendment; its extra prefill work remains unmeasured.
These findings apply to runtime-bounded, adapted workloads; differences in hardware, model, redacted inputs, and replay preparation bound their applicability to the original CacheWise evaluation.

For the recovery questions, we formulate a cost model that includes writes, reads, staging, and management, and specify matched comparisons of recomputation, DRAM restoration, and SSD restoration.
The proposed methods build from synchronous block offloading to asynchronous transfer and reuse-guided decisions.
This part remains an experimental agenda: the current artifacts contain uncached-request timings but no completed offloading comparison or validated SSD policy.
Accordingly, we report the replication findings and recovery framework without claiming an SSD speedup.
